01 / The question

Where you live can shape the environment around you.

I examined urban Michigan Census block groups and compared average relative land-surface heat across lower-, middle-, and higher-poverty groups. I also explored tree canopy as a separate, descriptive measure.

What's an “area”?

A Census block group—a small geographic unit, not an individual resident or necessarily a named neighborhood.

What is poverty here?

The percentage of residents living below 200% of the federal poverty threshold, not the usual 100% threshold.

What is “relative heat”?

A satellite-based land-surface indicator compared against an area's urban-average benchmark—not air temperature in °F or °C.

04 / Behind the numbers

What makes this more than a pretty chart?

I used analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine whether the three poverty groups differed in average relative surface heat. Choose the level of explanation that works for you.

In everyday language

I compared thousands of block groups, rather than just three locations. Average heat indicators increased from the lower- to the higher-poverty group. Under the model's assumptions, the statistical test indicated differences larger than would typically be expected from random sampling variation.

Tree canopy showed the opposite pattern, but I used canopy descriptively rather than as a second main statistical test.

Because nearby places often share conditions, I also examined whether the heat pattern appeared when comparing groups within the same urban areas.

For the statistics-minded

One-way ANOVA: F(2, 6254) = 470.2; p < .001. Eta-squared: η² = .131, meaning the three-group classification accounted for approximately 13.1% of observed relative-heat variation in this model.

Tukey HSD: all three pairwise group mean differences were statistically significant (adjusted p < .001).

Assumptions: group SDs approximately 5.52, 5.57, 5.27. Median-centered equal-variance check p = .248. Welch ANOVA: F(2, 4166.8) = 479.22, p < .001.

Within-area sensitivity: across 37 Michigan-focused urban areas, average high-minus-low heat difference +3.73 units, paired t(37) = 7.62, p < .001. This does not fully correct for spatial autocorrelation.

Not causal

Higher poverty was associated with higher relative heat; the study can't show poverty caused it.

Not air temperature

This is land-surface heat relative to each urban area, not degrees Fahrenheit/Celsius or an individual's exposure.

Not independent places

Nearby block groups may have similar characteristics. Ordinary ANOVA doesn't account for all spatial dependence.

05 / Sources & methods

Transparency matters.

Source data: American Forests Tree Equity Score (Michigan). The cleaned analytical dataset contains 6,257 urban Census block groups, after excluding 347 low-population entries from the original 6,604. The socioeconomic measures originate in the 2017–2021 ACS, and relative heat largely uses summer 2022 satellite imagery. This is a cross-sectional observational analysis.

The website's group and urban-area averages are calculated from the cleaned CSV. Michigan-wide poverty groups are ranked tertiles and are used consistently for local comparisons. The 37 selectable Michigan-focused urban areas each contain at least two lower- and two higher-poverty block groups. Local comparisons are descriptive and can differ from the overall statewide pattern. The cross-state Toledo urban area is included in the analytical comparison but excluded from the 37-area Michigan-focused selector.
